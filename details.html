    What each detail is made of

    The same parts, written out. Numbers match the pins on the model.

    Stone and mortar gravity wall

    1. Stone base with toe and heel. Solid stone set in mortar below grade. The toe sticks out in front of the face; the bottom slopes down toward the heel to lock into the soil.
    2. Battered face stone. Face stone per contract, laid to a batter so the face leans back into the hill. All face joints pointed.
    3. Fully mortared zone. Stone fully mortared top, bottom, front and back: behind the face, the bottom course, the top course and the back stones.
    4. Tightly fitted stone core. Interior stone packed tight. No mortar required in the core.
    5. Back stones, irregular back face. Mortared back course. The back face may be irregular, stepping in as the wall gets thinner toward the top.
    6. Cap stone. Cap stone along the top of the face. Openings for the PVC fence post sleeves are left in the top of the wall behind the cap.
    7. Weep pipes. Set just above final grade and spaced along the wall, running from the drainage stone out through the face. The back end is wrapped in filter fabric and banded.
    8. Drainage zone. Continuous gravel or clean free-draining rock behind the wall, sized for the wall. The back of the drainage zone leans away from the wall as it goes up, so the zone gets wider toward the top.
    9. Filter fabric around drainage zone. Black nonwoven geotextile, finely perforated so water passes through. Wraps the gravel so fines from the soil do not clog it, following the back of the drainage zone.
    10. Compacted soil below weep pipe. Compacted soil fills in under the drainage zone so water is pushed out the weeps.
    11. Clay cap from onsite soils. A layer of clay over the top of the wall and the drainage zone so runoff sheets over the top of the wall instead of soaking in.
    12. Retained soil. Natural or compacted fill behind the wall. Slope above the wall as shown on the plans.
    13. Final grade per civil. The soil in front of the wall provides passive pressure against the base and toe, helping hold the wall in place. Final grade per the civil plans.
    14. Fence if required. Posts set in PVC sleeves cast into the top of the wall, behind the cap, when a fence goes on the wall. The sleeve is shown around each post.

    Brick thinwall screen wall

    1. Drilled concrete piers. Round drilled piers under every column carry the wall down to stable soil.
    2. Pier cages. Four vertical bars inside round ties, with a double tie at the top of the pier.
    3. Pier hook bars. L-shaped bars at the top of each pier. The long leg laps down into the pier cage and the short leg turns into the pier cap or beam, tying the two together.
    4. Pier caps and grade beam. A deeper pier cap under each major column. Under the panels and minor columns, a small concrete beam at grade ties the piers together.
    5. Beam and pier cap reinforcing. The beam under the panels and minor columns has two continuous bars with ties. At the major columns the pier cap has three bars top and bottom with closed stirrups.
    6. Carton void form. Trapezoidal cardboard carton form under the beam between piers, so swelling clay has room and does not lift the wall.
    7. Single wythe thin brick panel. One wythe of thin brick in running bond, spanning column to column on the beam.
    8. Joint reinforcement. Galvanized wire laid in the mortar joints of the panel, running into the columns.
    9. Major column CMU cores. Each major column is hollow concrete block on the pier cap. The cells with bars are filled with concrete.
    10. Column vertical bars. Four bars in every column, two each side, from the cap or beam to the top of the column. The cells and cores with bars are filled with concrete.
    11. Stone veneer on the major columns. Stone veneer wraps the CMU core of each major column.
    12. Minor brick columns. Minor columns are brick, laid around a core that holds the bars and is filled with concrete.
    13. Caps. Brick cap along the panels, and caps on the minor and major columns to shed water.

    Entry monument sign wall

    1. Grade beam footing. One reinforced footing runs under the wall and both columns, the same width as the wall and the columns above it.
    2. Void boxes. Cardboard carton forms under the footing between the piers. They carry the wet concrete during the pour, then break down and leave a gap, so swelling clay cannot push up on the footing. The piers carry the load.
    3. Drilled concrete piers. Piers under each column and one at the center carry the monument down to stable soil and resist overturning from wind.
    4. Pier cages. Vertical bars tied inside hoops, running up out of each pier into the footing.
    5. Pier hook bars. L-shaped bars at the top of each pier. The long leg laps down into the pier cage and the short leg turns into the footing, tying the two together.
    6. Footing reinforcing. Top and bottom mats of bars each way, tied together with stirrups. Extra bars run through the wider footing under each column.
    7. Vertical bars in grouted cells. Bars hook into the footing with an L at the bottom and run up through the block. Every cell with a bar is grouted solid.
    8. CMU core. Hollow concrete block laid in running bond, with the cells over the bars grouted solid. This is the structure behind the stone.
    9. Natural stone veneer. Hand-set stone over the block on both faces and around the columns.
    10. Veneer ties. Galvanized ties anchor the stone to the block every few courses.
    11. Sign panel and letters. A cast stone or metal panel set into the stone, with the community name in raised letters.
    12. Cast stone caps. Caps on the wall and columns overhang the stone and shed water away from the core.

    Overhead entry feature

    1. Spread footings. Concrete footings sized for the uplift, sliding and bearing that wind puts on the long span.
    2. Footing and pedestal reinforcing. Top and bottom mats of bars each way in the footing. The pedestal verticals hook into the bottom mat and anchor the post, with closed ties at the top of the pedestal.
    3. Concrete pedestals. Pedestals bring each support above grade and hold the anchor rods.
    4. Steel posts. Steel posts at each support, set on base plates over the anchor rods.
    5. Post-to-truss connections. Bolted connections where each post picks up the truss.
    6. Gusset and splice plates. Plates at the post stations and at the field splices, so the truss can ship in pieces and bolt together on site.
    7. Top and bottom chords. Curved chords give the ribbon its shape and carry most of the bending.
    8. Side web diagonals. Diagonals on each face turn the chords into a truss.
    9. Plan bracing. Bracing in the top and bottom planes stiffens the ribbon sideways against wind.
    10. Verticals and struts. Members between the chords tie the truss faces together.
