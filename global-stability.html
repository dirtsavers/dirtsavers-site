How retaining walls fail

A retaining wall can slide, tip, sink, or get pushed out by water. Those are checks on the wall itself. The one that gets missed is global stability: the wall holds together and the ground it sits in moves.

Global stability

The wall passes. The ground doesn’t.

The checks above are about the wall. Global stability is about the hillside the wall sits in. The analysis looks at deep, curved surfaces that run behind the wall, under it and out through the slope below, and asks one question of each: does the soil along that curve hold, or does everything above it slide?

When it doesn’t hold, the wall can be in perfect shape and still end up at the bottom of the slope, with the yard, the fence and the pool deck going with it.

What it is and when to ask for it.

When a job needs one

Not every wall needs a full analysis. These are the conditions where we recommend one.

Steep slope belowGround that drops away in front of the wall, especially down to a pond, creek or channel.
Tiered wallsWalls stepped up a hill closer together than about twice the lower wall’s height act like one tall wall.
Tall wallsMore height means deeper slip surfaces and more load on the ground under the wall.
WaterDetention ponds, creeks, swales and high groundwater. Saturated soil can cut the safety margin by as much as half.
Loads near the topPools, houses, driveways, parking and fire lanes close to the top of the wall.
Soft or weak soilUndocumented fill, or a geotechnical report that shows soft layers deeper down.

Why it gets missed

Standard wall calculations stop at the wall and its footing. Wall plans often list global stability as “by others,” and on many jobs nobody else picks it up.

And there is nothing to see until it moves. The slip surface is underground, so the first signs show up in the yard, often after a wet season. By then the repair can cost many times what the analysis would have.

Running it right

A global stability run is only as good as what goes into it. These are what make the answer real.

  1. Soil from the boringsStrengths and layers from the geotechnical report, extended across the site the way the borings show them, not one assumed soil.
  2. Water where it will beGroundwater, pond levels and wet-season conditions, not a dry day.
  3. Real loads in real placesPools, houses, drives, fire lanes and upper walls where they actually sit, without stacking every worst case on top of each other.
  4. The whole slopeSurfaces behind, under and in front of the wall, through the slope below, with tiers checked together.
  5. Water lines and sprinklersUtility and irrigation plans checked against each wall, so the wet case sits where a leak would actually put water.
  6. A margin on every sectionA target factor of safety met at each section checked, and any fix checked by the same analysis.

Water lines and sprinklers

Know where the water comes from.

Saturated soil is one of the most common triggers for a global failure, and a lot of that water never fell as rain. It comes from a leaking water line or a sprinkler system running at the top of the wall.

So the review doesn’t stop at the grading plan. We check the civil utility plans and the landscape irrigation plan against the wall layout, find the lines that sit in the ground a slip surface would move through, and check those sections with the soil wet around the line.

You get a recommended utility setback for each wall and a plan exhibit showing where lines fall inside it. Where a line can move, moving it is often cheaper than deepening a footing. Where it can’t, the plans call for sleeves, trench plugs, a shutoff on the high side or drip irrigation near the wall.

If it comes up short

Common fixes are a deeper footing, a wider base, a flatter slope, more room between tiers, or better drainage. Each one is checked by the same analysis before it goes on the plans.

A deeper footing works when the weakest surface runs just under the base: setting the base below it forces any slip to go deeper and longer, through more ground that can hold it. How deep comes from the analysis, not a rule of thumb.

What you get

A sealed global stability analysis with the factor of safety for each section, for walls we design and for walls designed by others, plus fix options where a section comes up short. When the utility and irrigation plans are provided, it includes a utility setback exhibit and setback schedule for each wall.
