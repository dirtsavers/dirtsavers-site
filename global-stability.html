Failure modes

How retaining walls fail

A retaining wall can fail a few different ways. It can slide out at the base, tip over its toe, get blown out by water trapped behind it, sink into the ground under it, or ride down the hill with the ground it sits in. Every project is screened for all of these. How much detailed analysis each one needs depends on the wall and the site. Here is what a few of them look like, in plain terms.

Global stability

What global stability checks

A normal wall design checks the wall itself: that it will not slide, tip over or sink, and that the wall itself is strong enough. Global stability looks at a bigger piece of ground. It checks deep slip surfaces that run behind the wall and underneath it. The circular arc in the drawing is one example; the analysis looks at many possible surfaces.

If the soil along that surface cannot hold, the wall, the backfill and the yard above all move together as one block. The wall can be in perfect shape and still end up at the bottom of the slope.

Global stability in under two minutes

What it is and when to ask for it.

When a job should have one

Every project is screened for global stability. A detailed analysis is not needed for every wall; these are the conditions where we recommend one.

Steep slopesA slope above the wall, or a slope below it that drops away from the toe.
Tiered wallsTwo or more walls stepped up a hill, where the upper walls load the lower ones.
Tall wallsTaller walls reach deeper into the ground and put more load on it.
Water at the toeDetention ponds, creeks, channels and drainage swales along the base of the wall.
Loads near the topPools, houses, driveways, parking and fire lanes close to the top of the wall.
Soft or wet soilClay, undocumented fill, or a geotechnical report that shows weak layers or high groundwater.

Why it can fall through the cracks

The standard wall calculations stop at the wall and its footing. On many projects the wall plans list global stability as "by others," and sometimes nobody else picks it up.

There is also nothing to see until it moves. The slip surface is deep in the ground, so the first signs are usually a crack in the yard above the wall or a bulge at the toe, often after a wet season. At that point the repair can cost many times what the analysis would have.

Deepened footings on steep slopes

When the ground drops away steeply in front of a wall, the weakest slip surface often runs right under the base. One possible fix is to set the footing deeper, below that surface. It is one option among several, and whether it works has to be shown by the analysis for that site.

How deep is not a guess. The global analysis shows where the weak surfaces are, and we size the footing to get below them with the margin the design calls for. If the analysis shows a deeper footing is not enough on its own, a different fix is used.

What we provide

We run global stability for walls we design and for walls designed by others, using the soil data from the geotechnical report. You receive a sealed analysis with the factor of safety for each section we check.

If a section comes up short, we give you fix options along with it, such as a deepened footing sized and checked by the analysis, a wider base, a flatter slope, more space between tiers, or better drainage.
